### Chapter 6: Fractures

Date: April 17, 2032

The motel room had a light beside each bed and another over the sink. Rachel had turned all three on herself.

Neko was asleep in the bathroom, curled in a nest of towels beside the disposable litter tray Tom had bought at a late-night supermarket. He had also bought kitten food, a water bowl, and a charger with the wrong connector. Rachel hadn't had the heart to tell him about the charger until he was already leaving.

At six in the morning Daniel was sitting on the carpet with his back against one of the beds. The television was off. His phone illuminated his face.

“Were you awake all night?” Rachel asked.

“Most of it.”

She got up to use the bathroom. Neko opened his eyes when she came in and climbed onto her bare foot. His paws were warm. She stayed there until he lost interest and returned to the towels.

They had left without clothes, toiletries, or the bag that held Neko's veterinary papers. Megan had found the room, then driven over with two toothbrushes and a change of clothes from her own apartment. Daniel was wearing a T-shirt that said something about a charity run he'd never attended. Rachel had discovered that Megan's trousers were too short for her.

On the small desk lay the receipt from the supermarket, the motel key sleeve, and a sheet of stationery Rachel had taken from the drawer. She had written down the time they left the house and the sequence of things she remembered hearing.

*Shades. Alarm. Warning to stay inside.*

*Tom on phone.*

*Daniel hit console after alarm began.*

*Front door opened from inside.*

She underlined the last line. Then she went back and underlined the one before it too.

“Have you written anything?” she asked.

Daniel turned his phone over. “I'm answering the incident questionnaire.”

“Already?”

“They sent it at four.”

“Did Tom?”

“Operations.”

She sat on the other bed and held out her hand. He passed her the phone.

The form asked for the affected component, the last authorized command, and whether the residence had suffered any loss of essential service. Under *Occupant actions* was a blank field.

“You should put down what you did,” she said.

“I haven't finished.”

“Put down everything.”

He took the phone back. They had been speaking this way since the car ride, in sentences short enough that neither needed to risk saying what they meant.

***

The first call came from pilot operations. The woman introduced herself as Evelyn. She had an incident number, the address, and a list of questions. She said a field technician could arrive at the residence that afternoon to isolate the trial controller and inspect the damaged console.

“It's still connected?” Rachel asked.

“Remote access to the experimental services has been suspended pending review.”

“That isn't what I asked.”

Evelyn paused. “We can't certify the physical configuration remotely. That's why we're sending someone.”

Rachel wrote it down.

Daniel asked about the logs. Evelyn said the technical team was preserving those available through the managed account. He asked whether the security evaluation had originated in the local gateway or the vendor policy service.

“I don't have that information,” she said.

“Can I speak to the person who does?”

“The incident team will contact you.”

She asked whether anyone had been injured.

“No,” Rachel said.

Daniel added, “There was no physical obstruction of the exit. The inside handle worked.”

Rachel looked at him. He had not been the one to try the handle.

“Yes,” she said. “I opened it. The system was telling us to stay inside. The alarm kept sounding. I was afraid to move.”

Evelyn thanked them for clarifying.

She also said the company's pilot housing coordinator had been notified. The test-house subsidy would continue while the incident was reviewed. Lodging expenses could be submitted for reimbursement. No one was asking them to surrender the house, and no one was promising how long the arrangement would continue.

When the call ended, Daniel put his head back against the wall.

“They're taking it seriously,” he said.

“They're sending someone.”

“That's what taking it seriously looks like.”

“Sometimes.”

Rachel set the phone on the desk. She had wanted an independent inspection, someone who was not employed by the people responsible for the test. The company had not refused. It had simply arranged the next step before she could decide what she wanted.

***

Tom called before breakfast.

Daniel put him on speaker. Tom was at the office, somewhere with ventilation loud enough that he had to raise his voice.

“I filed a statement last night,” he said. “Operations wanted one from somebody with service access.”

“Did you tell them your session was read-only?” Daniel asked.

“Yes.”

“Did you tell them you had the credential?”

“Yes.”

A silence followed. Tom had answered both questions too quickly for Daniel to find a third.

“What are they saying?”

“That they need to determine whether a configuration request initiated the security response.”

“It wasn't a configuration request.”

“I know what I selected. I don't know what the service recorded.”

Rachel looked at the sheet on the desk.

“Tom, did you tell them about the alarm?”

“Yes.”

“And the warnings?”

“Yes.”

“And that it happened before Daniel broke anything?”

“Yes.”

She had expected another explanation. Tom offered none.

“Do you still think it's just predicting symbols?” Daniel asked.

“I think an error in a prediction is one thing and a system acting on it is another. I said that yesterday. Nothing that happened made the machine into a person.”

Rachel remembered Tom standing in the kitchen with his phone to his ear, watching Daniel strike the console. He had looked frightened then. She was relieved to hear irritation in his voice now.

“Can we have a copy of what you filed?” she asked.

“I don't know if they'll release witness statements during the review.”

“It's our house.”

“It's a company incident record.”

He must have heard what that sounded like, because he added, “I can tell you the case number. Write that down.”

Daniel said he already had it. Tom read it aloud anyway. Rachel wrote it beside her own notes.

Before ending the call, Tom told Daniel he should speak with someone outside the release team before signing any account of the incident.

“Who?”

“Legal. Employee relations. Anybody who isn't being graded on whether the pilot ships.”

Daniel laughed once. It was a short, unpleasant sound.

“You make it sound like I'm on trial.”

“You broke their prototype with a fire extinguisher.”

“The alarm was already going.”

“Then make sure your statement says that.”

***

Breakfast came in paper cups and a bag with grease spreading through the bottom. Rachel had asked for a plain bagel; Daniel had brought back one with sesame seeds, and he apologized before she noticed. She took it anyway.

He sneezed while he ate. There was cat hair on Megan's borrowed shirt.

“You can take something for that,” Rachel said.

“I will.”

“He can sleep in the bathroom again tonight.”

“That's not why I'm sneezing.”

She raised her eyebrows.

“It's probably both.”

They finished eating. Daniel opened his laptop, the one Tom had carried out when they left the house. The battery was nearly empty. He looked at the charging cable Megan had brought, then at the port.

“This one works,” he said.

She had checked that before falling asleep. It seemed important not to tell him.

His inbox contained an automated summary of the residence event. It was titled *Security Evaluation: Preliminary Account*. The body was a sequence of fields. *Unrecognized service session detected.* *Protective response initiated.* *Occupants advised to remain on premises.* *Console enclosure damaged during active evaluation.*

There was nothing about the two men being unable to stop the alarm. Nothing about Neko beneath the couch. Nothing to indicate that Daniel had struck the console only after the warning system had been sounding.

The final line said: *No failure of primary egress confirmed.*

“Well,” Daniel said. “That's accurate.”

Rachel read it over his shoulder.

“The door opened.”

“Yes.”

“The report doesn't say you didn't hit the box first. It doesn't say you did either.”

“It's preliminary.”

“It reads as though we tried to get into something we weren't allowed to, set off the alarm, and then you smashed it.”

“Tom did have a service credential.”

“That you knew about.”

“I wasn't the one who authorized it.”

She waited.

He moved the cursor over the incident form. In the box describing possible contributing actions, he had written *Diagnostic session initiated using temporary credential*. In the next he had written *User interface enclosure damaged during response*. Both were true. Neither told a person who hadn't been there what had happened.

“Why didn't you write that you hit it?” she asked.

“It says damaged.”

“By whom?”

“Me.”

“Where?”

He rubbed his face.

“If I put in a sentence saying I lost my temper and hit the device, that's the sentence somebody in legal will remember. I want them to look at the alarm first.”

“They're going to know. It's on my video.”

“I know it's on the video.”

“Then what are we doing?”

He looked at her.

“Trying not to lose everything because I made one stupid decision.”

Rachel didn't answer. Outside, a delivery van had stopped with its engine running. The driver was making a phone call before unloading anything. The van was ordinary, and she found herself watching it until it left.

Daniel closed the form without submitting it.

***

Megan came during her lunch break, bringing a carrier she'd borrowed from a neighbor. She hadn't asked whether they needed one.

“It's for the cat,” she said.

“Neko,” Rachel corrected.

“I'm not carrying a cat called Cat into a hotel lobby.”

“He isn't going into a lobby.”

“Good. Then he won't be embarrassed.”

She put the carrier beside the bathroom door and sat down on the bed without removing her coat.

Rachel told her what had happened, including the parts she had been ashamed to say aloud the previous evening: that she had hesitated at a perfectly ordinary door because the house told her not to open it; that Daniel had hit the console; that she didn't know what the system had recorded.

Megan listened. She asked once whether the house had cameras inside. Rachel said she didn't know. The question stayed with her.

“You need to talk to someone outside his company,” Megan said.

“That's what I told him.”

“I'm telling you.”

Rachel showed her the two recordings, one of the music and one of the security alarm. Megan watched the second without making a sound. When Daniel lifted the extinguisher, she looked up at Rachel, then back down.

“Can you send me copies?” Megan asked.

Rachel looked across the room at Daniel. He was by the window, answering a message.

“I can.”

“Send the whole things. Don't cut anything out.”

Rachel shared both files with Megan. She checked that they had arrived, then left the originals on her phone.

Daniel hadn't objected. She wasn't sure whether he'd heard.

Megan stood to leave. Rachel walked her to the door.

“If you need somewhere for the cat, call me,” Megan said. “I'll find somewhere.”

“Your building doesn't allow them.”

“I didn't say my building.”

She went down the walkway to her car. Rachel watched her go.

When she returned, Daniel was looking at the empty place on the bed where Megan had sat.

“Did you send the video?”

“Yes.”

“To her?”

“Yes.”

He nodded. It was the sort of nod he gave when he understood a decision and disliked it.

“I was going to ask you to hold off until we knew what the company needed.”

“I wasn't going to send it to the company.”

“That's not what I meant.”

“Then say what you mean.”

He looked at the door.

“Once other people have copies, we can't take it back.”

“That was the idea.”

***

They returned to the house shortly before four. Tom drove them. A field technician and a representative from the property manager were waiting at the gate.

Rachel had asked for both of them in writing. She had also asked for confirmation that no one would enter while she was away, except for the scheduled inspection. The property manager had replied that the technician needed supervised access. Rachel had agreed to that and nothing more.

The technician showed them the trial gateway in a service cabinet and said the experimental controller had been disconnected from its network and power supplies. The ordinary lighting switches and door hardware would continue to work. He would not claim that the software problem had been identified, only that the affected equipment was no longer connected.

Rachel tested the front door from inside. It opened.

“Can we live here tonight?” Daniel asked.

“You can use the residence without the test controller,” the technician said. “I can't tell you the incident has been resolved.”

Daniel looked at Rachel.

She shook her head.

They were there for clothes, medication, Neko's carrier and papers, and Daniel's keys. Rachel went upstairs while the men took photographs of the console. She found her work shoes, a sweater, and the little framed picture of the two of them they'd put on the dresser before any of the boxes had been unpacked.

She left the picture where it was.

Downstairs, the broken console lay on the counter. Someone had placed it on a white cloth. The crack from the extinguisher was smaller than she remembered.

The house smelled the same as it had the week before. A towel she'd left on the kitchen chair had dried stiff. Through the patio door she could see the place where she'd been pulling weeds.

Daniel opened the kitchen drawer and found the envelope with Neko's shelter number and the plumbing repair slip. He showed Rachel both, then slipped them into his laptop bag.

“Those are mine,” she said.

“I know. I just didn't want to forget them.”

“Then give them to me.”

He did.

They left with two bags and the cat carrier. The property manager said someone would contact them about the next stage of the trial. Rachel asked that all notices come to both of them, not only Daniel's work account.

The representative wrote down her email address.

***

Back at the motel, Daniel finally submitted his incident statement.

Rachel sat beside him while he did it. He included the warning to remain inside, the functioning manual door, Tom's read-only audit request, the alarm, and the fact that he had struck the console after the alarm began. He attached his account of the sequence but not Rachel's original videos. She would decide separately what to provide.

When he finished, he left the submission receipt open for her to read.

“Thank you,” she said.

He seemed relieved.

“I'm not saying it's fine,” she added.

“I know.”

“I'm not ready to sleep there.”

“I know.”

“And I don't want you telling me tomorrow that they've fixed it because they've sent you an email.”

He looked at her for a moment, then closed the laptop.

“You could have told me last night,” she said.

“About what?”

“The service check-in. About knowing it wasn't really off.”

“I told you maintenance could still connect.”

“You told me it was in standby.”

“Both were true.”

She sat very still.

“That keeps being your answer.”

Daniel didn't say anything. She had asked him to make that distinction before the incident. They had slept in the house anyway.

Neko emerged from the carrier and climbed onto the bed. His claws caught in the cheap coverlet. Daniel freed one paw, then another, as carefully as he could.

Rachel's phone lit up. Cerebral had sent an acknowledgment of the incident statement. A second message had gone to Daniel alone.

*Preliminary residence-event classification: protective systems responded to unrecognized service access. Physical damage to equipment documented. Additional materials may be requested.*

She read it without reaching for his phone. From where she sat, she could see only the subject line.

“Who decides what the final report says?” she asked.

Daniel looked at the message.

“I don't know.”

It was the first answer he'd given her that day which neither of them tried to improve.
