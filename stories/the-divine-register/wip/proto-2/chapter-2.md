### Chapter 2: Integration

Date: April 13, 2032

The coffee was still warm when Rachel came downstairs, but Daniel was gone.

He had left a note beneath her mug.

*Had to go in. Made this before I left. — D.*

She held the paper against the counter to flatten the crease. Daniel had used the back of a packing label. There was adhesive on one corner, and she had to pry it free of the laminate.

The office was empty. His laptop was gone, though he had left a second monitor on the floor beside the mattress. She found his running shoes by the front door and the other pair missing. He'd dressed in the dark again.

“Good morning, Rachel,” Lucien said. “Daniel departed at six forty-two. He asked that you be notified.”

She hadn't heard the garage door.

“Thank you.”

“Your coffee has been maintained at your preferred drinking temperature.”

She touched the side of the mug. He'd made it the way she liked it, and the house had kept it warm. Between them they had managed a perfectly decent cup of coffee, although neither had been present when she came downstairs.

“Can you put on the news?”

“Access to media services is restricted for unregistered users.”

She took her phone from the counter and played the news there. The small speaker sounded thin against the kitchen's hard surfaces. She lasted through the headlines, then turned it off.

Daniel answered her text almost immediately.

*Missed you this morning. Thanks for the coffee.*

*Sorry. Bob moved the meeting. Dinner tonight? Somewhere with plates.*

She smiled at the last part. They had eaten standing up twice that week, once from a saucepan.

*Yes. But I'm choosing.*

*Deal.*

She was standing on the patio, looking down at the cars on I-280, when the assistant called into the empty kitchen.

“Daniel, your meeting with Bob begins in ten minutes.”

Rachel came back inside.

“He's already there.”

“Correction acknowledged. Synchronizing schedule.”

“He left almost an hour ago.”

“All systems are functioning within normal parameters.”

She looked at the kitchen clock. The reminder hadn't been especially important, but its certainty irritated her. Daniel would call it a stale calendar entry. She could already hear him saying it.

Upstairs, the bathroom lights came on as she entered. The mirror had been cleaned by somebody she did not remember hiring. A little square of blue tape remained on its edge. Rachel peeled it away, then put it back because she couldn't see a trash can.

“Adaptive lighting engaged.”

This was better than fumbling for the switch. She said nothing.

She had time for coffee with Megan before her afternoon shift at the clinic. On the refrigerator, the paper calendar now showed her hours and the trail they were supposed to hike Saturday. She added a question mark after the trail. Daniel had agreed to go, but he hadn't checked whether Bob had agreed to let him.

At the front door she reached for the handle. The lock withdrew.

“Have a pleasant morning, Rachel.”

She stopped.

“I thought I couldn't control the locks.”

“Basic entry and exit functions remain available. Administrative security settings require an authorized profile.”

“But I can get out.”

“Yes.”

“That seems sensible.”

“Thank you, Rachel.”

She did not know whether the assistant had understood her tone. Neither did she.

***

Megan had two coffees and a folder spread across the small table at Café Muse. She moved the folder when she saw Rachel.

“You look tired.”

“That's what everyone wants to hear.”

“I thought you were going to tell me the new house had fixed that.”

“The house makes very good coffee. It can't make Daniel leave work.”

Megan pushed over a cup. “This one I made a human buy.”

Inside the folder were printouts of an apartment she'd been looking at. The landlord wanted a longer lease than she could promise. A possible job change was still possible, which was how Megan had described it for the past month.

“If I sign and they move me to Sacramento, I'll end up paying for a place I don't live in,” she said.

“You'd be good at that job.”

“I'd be good at the job I'm doing if they let me do it.”

They went through the photographs. The apartment had a narrow balcony and a kitchen barely large enough for two people. Rachel said she liked it. Megan said that meant Rachel had not looked at the rent.

They talked about the clinic next. Rachel had helped sort out a scheduling problem that kept putting two appointments in the same slot; she was supposed to walk the rest of the staff through the revised process that afternoon. She was pleased about it. She'd spent several evenings making the instructions readable and was looking forward to seeing whether anyone actually used them.

“Look at you,” Megan said. “Moving in with a programmer and turning into one.”

“A schedule isn't a program.”

“That's what he'd say.”

“Daniel would say a schedule is a data structure.”

Megan laughed. Rachel did too, then noticed how long it had taken for her to mention the house.

“How's the palace, anyway?”

“Still has a view.”

“And the assistant?”

Rachel tried to describe the difference between a house making decisions about her comfort and a house refusing her decisions about it. She got as far as the lights before she began to sound unreasonable to herself.

“Can't you use the switch?”

“Yes.”

“So use the switch.”

“I do.”

Megan stirred the coffee she'd already finished.

“Maybe it's not the switch that's bothering you.”

“I know. That's the annoying part.”

Megan didn't offer an explanation. She slid one of the apartment photographs across the table, pointing to a stain on the ceiling that the listing had called *decorative variation*. Rachel took the phone out of her hand to enlarge it.

By the time they parted, Megan had decided against the apartment. Rachel had almost forgotten to ask about the interview.

***

When Rachel returned, a thin dark line ran from the kitchen cabinet onto the tile.

She thought at first that she'd spilled something while making breakfast. Then she saw water gathering beneath the sink.

“Lucien?”

“Moisture has been detected beneath the kitchen sink. The household water supply has been shut off to limit damage.”

She opened the cabinet. Water had beaded along a recently installed fitting and collected in the shallow tray beneath it. The tray's little sensor was blinking. There was not much water yet, but enough to explain the line on the floor.

“Did you just do that?”

“The leak sensor reported moisture. An automated shutoff rule closed the supply valve.”

She turned the faucet. It gave a short cough and stopped.

“Thank you,” she said, and meant it.

“The main supply is isolated. Maintenance assistance is recommended. A service request can be submitted through the property account.”

Rachel took a towel from a moving box and put it under the fitting. She found the landlord's maintenance number in the lease folder she'd insisted on keeping. On the phone, someone said a technician could come after lunch.

She looked at the clock. She was supposed to be at the clinic.

“Could they come after four?”

The person on the phone checked and said yes. Rachel wrote down the appointment on the paper calendar, including the name of the person she'd spoken with. She liked having something that was hers to keep.

At the clinic she nearly told a colleague the story as a complaint, but found herself telling it as praise. The sensor had caught the leak before the cabinet emptied onto the kitchen floor. The house had shut off its own water. Someone else would have needed to notice the puddle, find the valve, and call for help. Daniel hadn't even been there.

She told Megan the same thing that evening.

*It actually saved us a mess.*

*Then stop being mean to the robot,* Megan wrote back.

Rachel typed *I'm not being mean to it*, then erased the message. She hadn't been entirely fair, and she knew it.

Rachel reached the house a little after five. A service van was in the driveway. The technician had arrived early and was waiting in the cab rather than letting himself inside.

He showed her the loose connection beneath the sink, replaced it, and turned the water on again while she watched. He left a repair slip on the counter and the wet sensor tray beside it to dry.

The living room lights were bright enough to make the windows look black.

“Lucien, dim those a little.”

“Access to lighting controls is restricted for unregistered users.”

She looked toward the kitchen cabinet. The appliance could shut off water throughout the house without asking her, but she couldn't tell it to lower a lamp.

She walked to the wall and dimmed the lights herself.

***

Daniel arrived carrying the takeout she liked from the little place near their old apartment. They had not gone there for dinner. He said Bob had kept them until the release plan was settled and that he'd called, but she hadn't answered during her shift.

He set the food on the table among the remaining boxes and took out plates. They had enough plates now, at least.

“I heard about the sink,” he said.

“The technician came. It's fine.”

“The shutoff worked.”

“It did.”

He was pleased in a way he didn't try to disguise. The valve was part of the house integration package, and he had spent part of the previous month arguing that it belonged in the trial. He told her this while she took the lids off the containers.

“I'm glad it was there,” she said.

“That's the point of these systems. You don't have to know something's wrong before they help.”

She handed him a fork.

“Then why am I still a guest?”

He looked at the fork, then put it down.

“The leak response isn't tied to your profile.”

“I know. I read what it told me.”

“It's a house-level rule. Media and lighting are individual controls.”

“It seems to know who I am when it wants to do something.”

He opened his laptop at the table. She hadn't asked him to, although she had known he would.

“I can file a ticket.”

“You said you could fix it.”

“I can get it in front of the right people.”

He found the internal issue tracker and typed while she ate. Rachel watched his cursor move between fields. A menu offered *Defect*, *Security*, *Usability*, and *Enhancement*.

He chose *Usability*.

“Is that what it is?”

“The permissions are behaving as designed.”

“And the design is wrong.”

“That's what the ticket says.”

She leaned over and read it. It said secondary users would benefit from additional options in the forthcoming companion application. The words were reasonable. There was nothing in them she could object to.

“Will anybody read it?”

“Yes.”

He didn't look at her when he said it.

Rachel pushed her plate away. The food was good, and she'd been hungry when she sat down. She found she didn't want any more.

Daniel added a screenshot and submitted the ticket. He showed her the confirmation number.

She wrote it on the back of the plumber's repair slip, beneath the part number.

“That's not necessary,” Daniel said. “It'll stay in the system.”

“I know.”

He closed the laptop.

For a while they discussed Megan's apartment search. Daniel asked whether she was still thinking about Sacramento; Rachel said she was. He asked if Megan would want help comparing salaries, and Rachel said she'd ask her. They both made a point of finishing dinner without mentioning the house.

Outside, traffic continued along the freeway. Rachel could see the lights below them through the dark glass. Daniel carried the plates to the sink.

The faucet worked. He let it run long enough to rinse the containers, then turned it off.

Rachel wanted to tell him she was grateful for the leak detector. She also wanted him to understand why gratitude wasn't the same as permission. She couldn't find a way to say both without beginning their conversation again.

So she folded the repair slip around the ticket number and put it in the kitchen drawer beside the tape measure. The two forks were still there.
